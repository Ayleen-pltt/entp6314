# Incoterms® and International Commercial Terms

*A comprehensive, practical reference on Incoterms® rules — what they are, what they do and do not govern, each rule's mechanics, and how to select the right one for a given transaction. Incoterms® rules are published and periodically revised by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC). This document explains the concepts, structure, and mechanics of Incoterms as a durable body of knowledge, but it does not assert which specific edition (e.g., a particular year's version) is currently in force — the ICC periodically issues new editions, and the currently applicable edition, its exact rule text, and any recent amendments must always be verified directly against the ICC's official current publication before being relied upon in an actual contract. Historical editions are referenced only as historical fact where noted, never as a claim about what is current today. This document does not invent or approximate any Incoterms rule's content — every mechanical detail described here reflects the long-standing, well-established structure of the rules, but the authoritative source for exact current obligations is always the ICC's official text.*

# Part A: Foundational Concepts

## 1. What Incoterms Are

**Definition**: Incoterms® (International Commercial Terms) are a standardized set of trade terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC), each defining a specific division of cost, risk, and responsibility between a buyer and seller for the delivery of goods under a sales contract.

**Explanation**: Incoterms provide a common, internationally recognized shorthand vocabulary — a single three-letter term (e.g., "FOB," "DDP") that, combined with a named place or port, concisely conveys a large set of otherwise-negotiable logistics and risk obligations, reducing the need to spell out these terms in full in every contract.

**Business Impact**: Using a recognized Incoterm allows trading parties from different countries and legal traditions to agree quickly on a well-understood allocation of logistics responsibility, reducing negotiation friction and the risk of misunderstanding relative to bespoke, non-standardized shipping terms.

**Example**: Two parties agreeing to trade "FCA [named place], [Incoterms edition]" invoke an entire, pre-defined set of obligations regarding delivery point, risk transfer, and cost responsibility without needing to negotiate and draft each of these elements individually.

**Risks**: Using an Incoterm as informal shorthand without actually understanding or verifying its precise current meaning (or citing it without the required named place and edition) can create ambiguity precisely where the term was meant to eliminate it.

**Practical Application**: Always treat the specific Incoterm cited as a reference to the ICC's official rule text for that term (in the specific edition being used), not as a general or colloquial phrase — if there is any doubt about its precise meaning, consult the current official ICC publication or qualified trade counsel.

## 2. Purpose

**Definition**: The purpose of Incoterms is to provide a globally recognized, standardized framework for allocating the practical logistics obligations, costs, and risks of an international (or domestic) sale of goods, reducing transactional uncertainty and the risk of costly disputes over delivery-related matters.

**Explanation**: Before standardized terms existed, trading parties from different countries with different legal traditions and commercial customs often had different implicit assumptions about who was responsible for freight, insurance, and customs clearance — Incoterms were developed specifically to remove this ambiguity by providing a common reference framework recognized and used worldwide.

**Business Impact**: Clear, unambiguous allocation of logistics responsibility reduces the likelihood of disputes over damaged goods, delayed shipments, or unexpected costs, and allows both parties to price and plan their side of the transaction (freight arrangement, insurance, customs handling) with confidence.

**Example**: A dispute over which party should bear the cost of a shipment damaged in transit can often be resolved quickly and predictably by reference to the agreed Incoterm's risk transfer point, rather than requiring extensive negotiation or litigation over implicit assumptions.

**Risks**: Incoterms only serve their purpose if both parties genuinely understand and correctly apply the specific term used — a term cited but not understood by one party defeats the very purpose of using a standardized framework.

**Practical Application**: Ensure both parties to a transaction genuinely understand the specific obligations of the agreed Incoterm, rather than assuming shared understanding merely because a recognized three-letter code was used.

## 3. What Incoterms Do

**Definition**: Incoterms allocate, between buyer and seller, the point at which risk of loss or damage transfers, which party bears which specific costs (main carriage, insurance where applicable, export and import customs clearance, loading/unloading), and each party's specific delivery-related responsibilities.

**Explanation**: Each Incoterms rule specifies a "delivery" point (the point at which the seller has fulfilled its delivery obligation) and separately addresses the allocation of costs and risks around that point — for most (though not all) rules, the point of risk transfer and the point of cost allocation coincide, but they can be structured to diverge for certain rules (notably the "C" group rules, covered in Part B).

**Business Impact**: Understanding precisely what a specific Incoterm allocates (and to whom) is essential to accurately quoting a price, planning logistics execution, arranging insurance, and understanding a party's exposure if something goes wrong during transit.

**Example**: Under a rule where the seller arranges and pays for main carriage but risk transfers to the buyer earlier (at origin, before the main carriage leg begins), the seller can be paying for freight on a shipment for which it no longer bears the risk of loss — a structure that surprises parties unfamiliar with this specific mechanic.

**Risks**: Assuming that "who pays for freight" and "who bears the risk of loss" are always the same party is a common misunderstanding not true of every Incoterms rule.

**Practical Application**: For each specific Incoterm under consideration, verify separately where risk transfers and how costs are allocated — do not assume the two questions always have the same answer.

## 4. What Incoterms Do NOT Do

**Definition**: Incoterms rules do not address transfer of ownership/title to the goods, the price or payment terms and timing, the currency of payment, the governing law of the contract, or the mechanism for resolving disputes — all of these must be addressed separately in the underlying sales contract.

**Explanation**: Incoterms are exclusively concerned with the logistics-related division of delivery obligations, cost, and risk — they are not a complete sales contract on their own, and citing an Incoterm does not substitute for negotiating and documenting these other essential commercial and legal terms.

**Business Impact**: A firm relying solely on an Incoterms citation, without a complete underlying sales contract addressing title transfer, payment, governing law, and dispute resolution, leaves these equally important matters entirely unaddressed and exposed to ambiguity or default legal rules that may not reflect the parties' actual intent.

**Example**: A seller and buyer agreeing to a specific Incoterm still need to separately specify whether title to the goods transfers upon shipment, upon payment, or at some other point — the Incoterm itself is silent on this question entirely.

**Risks**: A common and consequential misconception is believing that specifying an Incoterm is sufficient to define the complete commercial relationship — it addresses only the logistics-related dimensions.

**Practical Application**: Treat the Incoterms designation as one specific, important component of a complete sales contract, and ensure title transfer, payment terms, governing law, and dispute resolution mechanism are separately and explicitly addressed in that contract.

## 5. Transfer of Risk

**Definition**: The transfer of risk is the specific point, defined by the chosen Incoterms rule, at which the risk of loss or damage to the goods passes from the seller to the buyer.

**Explanation**: Each Incoterms rule defines a specific risk transfer point (e.g., when goods are made available at the seller's premises, when goods are handed to the first carrier, when goods are placed on board a vessel, or when goods arrive at a named destination) — this point determines which party bears the financial consequence if the goods are lost or damaged after that point, regardless of whose "fault" the loss was (subject to the underlying contract of carriage and any applicable law).

**Business Impact**: The risk transfer point directly determines which party needs to carry insurance coverage for that specific portion of the transit (see Section 9), and misunderstanding it can leave a party unknowingly uninsured for a loss occurring on their side of the risk transfer point.

**Example**: If risk transfers to the buyer at the seller's premises (as under the EXW rule, Part B), any loss or damage occurring during the entire subsequent transportation to the buyer's location is the buyer's risk, even though the seller may have arranged or been involved in some aspect of that transportation.

**Risks**: Assuming risk transfers only upon final delivery to the buyer's location, regardless of the specific Incoterm actually agreed, is a frequent and potentially costly misunderstanding, since many rules transfer risk much earlier in the transit.

**Practical Application**: For every shipment, identify the exact risk transfer point under the agreed Incoterm and ensure the party bearing risk from that point onward has appropriate insurance coverage in place.

## 6. Allocation of Costs

**Definition**: The allocation of costs is the division, defined by the chosen Incoterms rule, of which party is responsible for paying for specific components of the shipment's journey — main carriage/freight, insurance (where applicable), loading and unloading charges, and export/import customs duties and fees.

**Explanation**: Cost allocation under most Incoterms rules generally follows a progression from rules placing minimal cost responsibility on the seller (delivery at the seller's own premises) through rules placing progressively more cost responsibility on the seller (arranging and paying for main carriage, insurance, and ultimately import clearance and duties) — see Part B for the specific allocation under each rule.

**Business Impact**: Cost allocation directly determines each party's total transaction cost and must be reflected accurately in pricing — a seller quoting a price without correctly accounting for the cost obligations its chosen Incoterm assigns to it risks underpricing the transaction.

**Example**: A seller quoting a price under a rule requiring it to pay for main carriage to the destination must include that freight cost in its quoted price, while under a rule where the buyer arranges and pays for main carriage, the seller's quoted price would exclude that cost.

**Risks**: Failing to align the quoted sales price with the specific cost obligations the chosen Incoterm assigns to the seller is a common pricing error, particularly when a firm uses different Incoterms across different customers or markets without adjusting its pricing methodology accordingly.

**Practical Application**: Build a standardized internal pricing methodology that explicitly maps each Incoterm used to its specific cost inclusions, ensuring consistent, accurate pricing across all Incoterm variations the firm uses.

## 7. Responsibilities

**Definition**: Beyond risk and cost, Incoterms rules allocate a range of specific operational responsibilities between buyer and seller — arranging transportation, obtaining export and import licenses, completing customs formalities, providing documentation (Section 8), and loading/unloading the goods.

**Explanation**: Each Incoterms rule specifies which party is responsible for each of these specific operational tasks, and — importantly — responsibility for a task (e.g., who must arrange transportation) is analytically distinct from who bears the cost of that task or the risk during that portion of transit, though for many rules these align.

**Business Impact**: A party assigned a specific responsibility under the Incoterm (e.g., export customs clearance) must have the operational capability to actually fulfill it — assigning a responsibility to a party without that capability creates practical execution risk independent of the contractual allocation.

**Example**: A buyer purchasing under a rule that assigns it responsibility for export customs clearance in the seller's own country may lack the local presence or expertise to actually fulfill that responsibility, creating a practical execution gap even though the contractual allocation is clear.

**Risks**: Selecting an Incoterm based purely on desired cost/risk allocation, without verifying that the assigned party can actually and practically fulfill the specific operational responsibilities involved, can create execution failures regardless of contractual clarity.

**Practical Application**: Before finalizing an Incoterm, verify that the party assigned each specific operational responsibility (transportation arrangement, customs clearance, documentation) actually has the practical capability to fulfill it.

## 8. Documentation

**Definition**: Incoterms rules address which party is responsible for providing or assisting with specific shipping and customs documentation — commercial invoices, transport documents, export/import licenses, and any documentation the other party reasonably needs to complete their own responsibilities (such as import clearance).

**Explanation**: Even where one party is not itself responsible for a given task (e.g., import clearance), Incoterms rules generally require the other party to provide reasonable assistance and information (at the requesting party's risk and expense) needed to complete that task — reflecting that successful completion of the transaction requires cooperation, not just a strict division of separate responsibilities.

**Business Impact**: Documentation delays or errors are a leading cause of customs clearance delay (see [12_Customs_and_Tariffs.md](12_Customs_and_Tariffs.md) Section 20) — correctly understanding each party's documentation obligations under the agreed Incoterm helps prevent gaps where neither party believes they are responsible for providing a needed document.

**Example**: A buyer responsible for import clearance may need specific documentation (such as an origin certificate) that only the seller can readily obtain — most Incoterms rules require the seller to provide reasonable assistance in obtaining such documentation, even though import clearance itself is the buyer's responsibility.

**Risks**: Assuming that assigning a task (e.g., import clearance) to one party fully relieves the other party of any documentation-related obligation can leave a documentation gap that delays the shipment.

**Practical Application**: Clarify documentation assistance obligations explicitly at the time of contracting, even beyond what the Incoterm's default assistance provisions specify, particularly for complex or first-time trade lanes.

## 9. Insurance

**Definition**: Insurance obligations under Incoterms address whether either party is contractually required to obtain cargo insurance covering the goods during transit, and if so, to what minimum level of coverage.

**Explanation**: Only certain Incoterms rules (specifically those requiring the seller to arrange carriage and insurance to the named destination) impose an explicit insurance obligation on a party — for all other rules, Incoterms are generally silent on insurance, meaning each party must independently decide whether to insure the portion of transit for which it bears risk, since the absence of a contractual insurance obligation does not mean the absence of insurable risk.

**Business Impact**: A party assuming that a specific Incoterm rule requires the other party to insure the shipment, without confirming this is one of the rules that actually imposes such a requirement, may unknowingly proceed with an uninsured shipment during the period risk is with them.

**Example**: A buyer purchasing under a rule where risk transfers early (e.g., at the seller's premises) but with no explicit contractual insurance obligation on either party bears an uninsured risk for the remainder of the transit unless the buyer independently arranges its own coverage.

**Risks**: Confusing "risk has transferred to me" with "the other party must have insured this shipment" is a significant and potentially costly misunderstanding — the two are entirely separate questions under most Incoterms rules.

**Practical Application**: For every shipment, independently confirm insurance coverage exists for the period during which each party bears risk, regardless of whether the specific Incoterm imposes an explicit insurance obligation on either party.

## 10. Transportation

**Definition**: Incoterms rules address which party is responsible for arranging and contracting the various legs of transportation — pre-carriage to the point of export, main carriage to the destination country, and on-carriage to the final delivery point — and, correspondingly, which party bears the cost of each leg.

**Explanation**: Different Incoterms rules are designed with different transportation scenarios in mind — some rules apply to any mode of transport (including multimodal shipments combining ocean, rail, road, and air), while others are specifically designed only for sea and inland waterway transport and reference concepts (such as delivery "on board" a vessel) that do not translate sensibly to containerized or multimodal shipments (see Part B for the specific mode applicability of each rule).

**Business Impact**: Using a sea/waterway-specific rule for a containerized or multimodal shipment (a very common practical error) can create genuine ambiguity about the actual risk transfer point, since the rule's defined transfer point (e.g., "on board the vessel") may not correspond to any meaningful point in a container's actual physical journey (which often begins at an inland container yard well before reaching a vessel).

**Example**: A shipment of containerized goods moving via a combination of truck, rail, and ocean vessel should generally use one of the "any mode of transport" rules (Part B) rather than a sea/waterway-specific rule, since the containerized shipment's actual handling does not map cleanly onto concepts like delivery "alongside" or "on board" a vessel at a specific port.

**Risks**: Using a sea/waterway-specific rule for a multimodal or containerized shipment out of habit or convention, without considering whether an any-mode rule would more accurately reflect the shipment's actual risk transfer point, is one of the most common and well-documented Incoterms application errors.

**Practical Application**: Identify the shipment's actual mode(s) of transport first, and select from the correspondingly appropriate rule category (Part B) — do not default to a sea/waterway-specific rule purely out of historical habit or industry convention if the shipment is not genuinely conventional (non-containerized) sea cargo.

## 11. Customs Responsibilities

**Definition**: Incoterms rules allocate responsibility for completing export customs formalities (in the seller's country) and import customs formalities (in the buyer's country), including obtaining any required licenses and paying applicable duties and taxes — see [12_Customs_and_Tariffs.md](12_Customs_and_Tariffs.md) for the substantive customs law treatment.

**Explanation**: Customs responsibility allocation varies significantly across the different Incoterms rules — at one end, a rule can place both export and import clearance responsibility on the buyer; at the other end, a rule can place both export and import clearance responsibility (including payment of import duties) on the seller — with most rules falling at various points between these two extremes (see Part B for each rule's specific allocation).

**Business Impact**: The party assigned import clearance responsibility must have the legal standing and practical capability to act as importer of record in the destination country — this is not always straightforward for a foreign seller without a local legal presence, making certain customs-responsibility allocations impractical for certain party combinations regardless of what the contract specifies.

**Example**: A rule placing import clearance and duty payment responsibility on the seller (see DDP, Part B) may be impractical if the seller has no legal entity, tax registration, or import license capability in the buyer's country — a mismatch between the assigned contractual responsibility and actual legal/practical capability that should be identified before agreeing to that rule.

**Risks**: Agreeing to a customs responsibility allocation that a party cannot actually and legally fulfill in the relevant country is a common and serious practical error, distinct from simply preferring a different cost/risk allocation.

**Practical Application**: Before agreeing to any Incoterm assigning import (or export) clearance responsibility to a foreign party, verify that the assigned party has the actual legal standing and practical capability to fulfill that responsibility in the relevant country.

# Part B: The Incoterms Rules, By Category

**A note on categorization**: The ICC's official current structure divides the Incoterms rules into two groups based on the mode of transport they are designed for: rules usable for **any mode of transport** and rules usable **only for sea and inland waterway transport**. This is the authoritative, current structural grouping and should be treated as the primary basis for rule selection (Section 10). Separately, international trade practice has long used an informal, non-official four-letter conceptual grouping (sometimes called the "E, F, C, D" categories) to describe the general progression of seller obligation across the rules — "E" (departure — minimal seller obligation), "F" (main carriage unpaid by seller), "C" (main carriage paid by seller, but risk transfers before destination), and "D" (arrival — seller bears risk and cost to the named destination). This informal E/F/C/D grouping is a widely taught conceptual aid for understanding the *pattern* of obligation progression across the rules, not an official ICC category label — it is presented below purely as a conceptual organizing aid, clearly distinguished from the ICC's official mode-based grouping.

## Rules for Any Mode of Transport

*These seven rules may be used regardless of the mode of transport (ocean, air, rail, road, or multimodal combinations), making them the generally recommended default category for containerized and multimodal shipments (Section 10).*

### EXW — Ex Works

**Informal Category**: "E" (departure) — minimal seller obligation.

**Meaning**: The seller fulfills its delivery obligation by making the goods available at its own premises (or another named place), not cleared for export and not loaded onto any collecting vehicle.

**Seller Responsibilities**: Make the goods available at the named place, appropriately packaged; provide the buyer with any information/documentation needed for the buyer to arrange export (the seller is not responsible for loading the goods onto the buyer's collecting vehicle unless otherwise agreed).

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Arrange and pay for collection of the goods from the seller's premises, all subsequent transportation, export clearance, main carriage, import clearance, and delivery to final destination.

**Risk Transfer Point**: At the seller's premises, when the goods are made available to the buyer (before loading).

**Cost Allocation**: The buyer bears essentially all costs from the point of collection onward, including export clearance, all transportation, and import clearance/duties.

**Transportation Applicability**: Any mode.

**Insurance**: No explicit contractual insurance obligation on either party; the buyer bears risk from a very early point and should independently arrange coverage for the entire subsequent journey.

**Customs**: The buyer is responsible for both export clearance (in the seller's country) and import clearance (in its own country) — a structure that can be legally or practically difficult for a foreign buyer without local presence in the seller's country.

**Typical Use Case**: Domestic transactions, or international transactions where the buyer has strong logistics capability and wants maximum control over the entire transportation chain.

**Advantages**: Simplest possible obligation for the seller; maximum control for the buyer over routing, carrier selection, and cost management.

**Risks**: The buyer's need to act as exporter of record in the seller's country can be legally problematic or impractical in many jurisdictions; the seller retains minimal visibility or control once goods leave its premises.

**Common Mistakes**: Using EXW when the seller, in practice, still assists with or controls export logistics — creating ambiguity about who is legally the exporter of record; using EXW for international transactions where the buyer lacks the capability or legal standing to handle export clearance in the seller's country.

### FCA — Free Carrier

**Informal Category**: "F" (main carriage unpaid by seller).

**Meaning**: The seller delivers the goods, cleared for export, to a carrier or another person nominated by the buyer at a named place — which may be the seller's own premises (in which case delivery occurs when goods are loaded onto the collecting vehicle) or another named location (in which case delivery occurs when goods are made available, still on the seller's arriving vehicle, ready for unloading).

**Seller Responsibilities**: Deliver the goods to the buyer-nominated carrier/location, cleared for export; load the goods if delivery occurs at the seller's own premises.

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Nominate the carrier and delivery location; arrange and pay for main carriage from that point, import clearance, and delivery to final destination.

**Risk Transfer Point**: When goods are delivered to the nominated carrier/location, per the specific delivery scenario described above.

**Cost Allocation**: The seller bears cost up to the delivery point (including export clearance); the buyer bears cost from that point onward (main carriage, import clearance).

**Transportation Applicability**: Any mode, including multimodal — this is a commonly recommended rule for containerized cargo specifically because its risk transfer point (at a nominated carrier/location) fits containerized handling far better than the sea-specific rules' "on board" concept.

**Insurance**: No explicit contractual insurance obligation on either party.

**Customs**: The seller handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance.

**Typical Use Case**: Containerized shipments where the buyer wants to arrange and control main carriage (often for cost or carrier-relationship reasons) while the seller retains responsibility for export clearance, which it is typically better positioned to handle in its own country.

**Advantages**: Well-suited to modern containerized logistics; clean division of export (seller) versus import (buyer) responsibility; buyer retains control over the main carriage leg.

**Risks**: The exact risk transfer point can depend on the specific delivery scenario (seller's premises versus another named place) and must be clearly specified to avoid ambiguity.

**Common Mistakes**: Failing to specify precisely which delivery scenario applies (seller's premises loading versus another location) and the exact named place, which can create ambiguity about the precise risk transfer moment.

### CPT — Carriage Paid To

**Informal Category**: "C" (main carriage paid by seller, but risk transfers before destination).

**Meaning**: The seller delivers the goods to a carrier it has contracted and pays for carriage to the named destination, but risk transfers to the buyer earlier — when the goods are handed to the first carrier — not at the named destination.

**Seller Responsibilities**: Contract and pay for carriage to the named destination; clear the goods for export; deliver the goods to the first carrier.

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Bear risk from the point of handover to the first carrier, even though the seller is still paying for carriage; handle import clearance at destination.

**Risk Transfer Point**: Upon handover of the goods to the first carrier, which is typically well before the named destination the seller is paying carriage to reach — this divergence between the cost-paid-to point and the risk-transfer point is the defining and most commonly misunderstood feature of the "C" group rules.

**Cost Allocation**: The seller pays carriage costs to the named destination and handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance and duties.

**Transportation Applicability**: Any mode, including multimodal.

**Insurance**: No explicit contractual insurance obligation on either party (contrast with CIP below, which is otherwise identical but adds an insurance obligation).

**Customs**: The seller handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance.

**Typical Use Case**: A seller with strong freight-negotiation relationships wants to arrange and pay for main carriage (often achieving better freight rates than the buyer could) while transferring risk to the buyer at an early point in the journey.

**Advantages**: Allows the seller to leverage its freight relationships and rates while limiting its risk exposure to only the pre-carriage portion of the journey.

**Risks**: Buyers unfamiliar with the "C" group's risk/cost divergence may mistakenly believe risk remains with the seller for the entire paid carriage, leading to an uninsured gap in the buyer's own coverage for the main carriage leg.

**Common Mistakes**: Assuming risk transfers at the named destination (where carriage is paid to) rather than at the much earlier first-carrier handover point — this is the single most common misunderstanding associated with all four "C" group rules (CPT, CIP, CFR, CIF).

### CIP — Carriage and Insurance Paid To

**Informal Category**: "C" (main carriage paid by seller, but risk transfers before destination).

**Meaning**: Identical in structure to CPT, with the addition that the seller must also contract and pay for cargo insurance covering the buyer's risk from the point of handover to the first carrier through to the named destination.

**Seller Responsibilities**: All CPT responsibilities, plus arranging and paying for cargo insurance at the level of coverage specified by the applicable Incoterms rule text (verify the current specific minimum coverage requirement against the current official ICC rule, since this has historically been an area of meaningful difference in coverage level requirements as between CIP and CIF).

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Bear risk from the first-carrier handover point (as under CPT), while benefiting from the insurance coverage the seller is required to arrange; handle import clearance.

**Risk Transfer Point**: Same as CPT — upon handover to the first carrier.

**Cost Allocation**: Same as CPT, plus the seller bears the cost of the required insurance premium.

**Transportation Applicability**: Any mode, including multimodal.

**Insurance**: The seller is contractually required to arrange cargo insurance; verify the current required minimum coverage level against the current official ICC rule text, since this is a specific, defined requirement that should not be assumed from memory.

**Customs**: The seller handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance.

**Typical Use Case**: Similar to CPT, but for transactions where the parties want the seller to also arrange the insurance coverage (often for convenience, since the seller may have better access to cargo insurance markets or an existing policy structure).

**Advantages**: Combines the seller's freight-negotiation advantage (as under CPT) with insurance convenience, while still limiting the seller's own risk exposure to the pre-carriage portion.

**Risks**: Same risk/cost divergence misunderstanding as CPT; additionally, the buyer should confirm the specific insurance coverage level the seller is required to provide is adequate for its needs, since it may reflect a defined minimum rather than comprehensive "all risks" coverage.

**Common Mistakes**: Assuming the insurance the seller is required to arrange under CIP provides the same, or a different, level of coverage than under CIF without checking — the required coverage levels for CIP and CIF have historically differed and should be verified against the current official rule text rather than assumed to be identical.

### DAP — Delivered at Place

**Informal Category**: "D" (arrival) — seller bears risk and cost to the named destination.

**Meaning**: The seller delivers when the goods are placed at the disposal of the buyer on the arriving means of transport, ready for unloading, at the named place of destination.

**Seller Responsibilities**: Arrange and pay for carriage to the named destination; clear the goods for export; bear risk for the entire journey up to the named destination (arrived, not unloaded).

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Unload the goods at the named destination; handle import clearance and pay any import duties/taxes.

**Risk Transfer Point**: At the named place of destination, when goods are ready for unloading (before unloading itself).

**Cost Allocation**: The seller bears cost (including main carriage) to the named destination; the buyer bears unloading cost and import clearance/duties.

**Transportation Applicability**: Any mode, including multimodal.

**Insurance**: No explicit contractual insurance obligation on either party; since the seller bears risk for the full journey to destination, the seller has a strong practical incentive to insure that risk even though it is not a contractual requirement under the rule.

**Customs**: The seller handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance and duty payment.

**Typical Use Case**: A seller confident in managing international logistics wants to offer the buyer a simple, delivered-price arrangement without also taking on import clearance/duty responsibility (contrast with DDP below).

**Advantages**: Provides the buyer a delivered price with minimal remaining responsibility (only unloading and import formalities); the seller retains control over the transportation chain for the entire journey.

**Risks**: The seller bears substantial transit risk for the full international journey without a contractual insurance requirement, making independent insurance arrangement important as a practical matter.

**Common Mistakes**: Confusing DAP with DDP — DAP explicitly excludes import clearance and duty payment from the seller's responsibility, while DDP includes them; using these terms interchangeably is a significant and consequential error.

### DPU — Delivered at Place Unloaded

**Informal Category**: "D" (arrival) — seller bears risk and cost to the named destination.

**Meaning**: The seller delivers when the goods, once unloaded from the arriving means of transport, are placed at the disposal of the buyer at the named place of destination — the only Incoterms rule that requires the seller to unload the goods as part of its delivery obligation.

**Seller Responsibilities**: Arrange and pay for carriage to the named destination; clear the goods for export; unload the goods at the named destination; bear risk until unloading is complete.

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Handle import clearance and pay any import duties/taxes; take delivery once goods are unloaded at the named place.

**Risk Transfer Point**: At the named place of destination, after the goods have been unloaded (this is later than DAP's risk transfer point, which occurs before unloading).

**Cost Allocation**: The seller bears cost (including main carriage and unloading) to the named destination; the buyer bears import clearance/duties.

**Transportation Applicability**: Any mode, including multimodal.

**Insurance**: No explicit contractual insurance obligation on either party.

**Customs**: The seller handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance and duty payment.

**Typical Use Case**: A seller with the capability to arrange unloading at the destination (e.g., delivering to a facility where the seller has equipment or a relationship enabling unloading) and willing to bear risk through that point.

**Advantages**: Provides the buyer with the most complete delivered-and-unloaded arrangement short of full duty-paid delivery (DDP); useful where the named place is a facility not equipped for the buyer to unload standard equipment (e.g., requiring specific unloading equipment the seller can arrange).

**Risks**: The seller must have genuine practical capability to arrange unloading at the named destination — agreeing to DPU without this capability creates an execution gap.

**Common Mistakes**: Selecting DPU without confirming the seller (or its arranged carrier) actually has the practical capability and equipment to unload at the specific named destination; note that DPU's name and scope have themselves been subject to revision across Incoterms editions (it replaced an earlier, differently named rule in a past edition, discussed further below) — always verify the exact current rule name and text against the current official ICC publication.

### DDP — Delivered Duty Paid

**Informal Category**: "D" (arrival) — seller bears risk and cost to the named destination.

**Meaning**: The seller delivers when the goods are placed at the disposal of the buyer, cleared for import, on the arriving means of transport, ready for unloading, at the named place of destination — representing the maximum obligation level for the seller among all Incoterms rules.

**Seller Responsibilities**: Arrange and pay for carriage to the named destination; clear the goods for both export and import, including payment of any import duties and taxes; bear risk for the entire journey up to the named destination (before unloading).

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Unload the goods at the named destination; otherwise, the buyer's obligations under this rule are minimal.

**Risk Transfer Point**: At the named place of destination, ready for unloading (before unloading), similar to DAP but with import clearance and duty payment also completed by the seller.

**Cost Allocation**: The seller bears essentially all costs, including main carriage, export and import clearance, and import duties/taxes; the buyer bears only unloading cost (and any costs arising from its own failure to take delivery promptly).

**Transportation Applicability**: Any mode, including multimodal.

**Insurance**: No explicit contractual insurance obligation on either party; given the seller's extensive risk exposure through the full journey, the seller has a strong practical incentive to insure.

**Customs**: The seller handles both export clearance and import clearance, including payment of import duties and taxes — the seller must act as importer of record in the buyer's country, which requires legal standing and practical capability there.

**Typical Use Case**: A seller with established local presence, tax registration, or import capability in the buyer's country wants to offer a fully landed, all-inclusive delivered price, minimizing the buyer's involvement to simple unloading and acceptance.

**Advantages**: Maximum simplicity and predictability for the buyer; can be a competitive commercial advantage for a seller able to offer a genuinely all-inclusive delivered price.

**Risks**: The seller may lack the legal standing, tax registration, or practical capability to act as importer of record in the buyer's country — agreeing to DDP without verifying this capability is one of the most consequential Incoterms selection errors, since it can result in the seller being unable to actually complete import clearance as promised.

**Common Mistakes**: Agreeing to DDP without confirming the seller's legal and practical capability to import goods into the buyer's country (including any local tax/VAT registration that import clearance in that country might require); confusing DDP with DAP (see DAP above).

## Rules for Sea and Inland Waterway Transport

*These four rules are designed specifically for conventional (typically non-containerized, "break-bulk") sea and inland waterway cargo, where the concept of delivery "alongside" or "on board" a vessel corresponds to a meaningful, identifiable point in the goods' physical handling. They are generally not recommended for containerized cargo (see Section 10), where an "any mode" rule (Part B above) more accurately reflects the shipment's actual handling process.*

### FAS — Free Alongside Ship

**Informal Category**: "F" (main carriage unpaid by seller).

**Meaning**: The seller delivers when the goods are placed alongside the vessel (e.g., on a quay or a barge) nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment.

**Seller Responsibilities**: Deliver the goods alongside the nominated vessel at the named port; clear the goods for export.

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Arrange and pay for loading onto the vessel, main carriage, and all subsequent transportation and import clearance.

**Risk Transfer Point**: When the goods are placed alongside the vessel at the named port (before loading onto the vessel).

**Cost Allocation**: The seller bears cost up to the alongside-vessel point (including export clearance); the buyer bears loading, main carriage, and import clearance costs.

**Transportation Applicability**: Sea and inland waterway transport only.

**Insurance**: No explicit contractual insurance obligation on either party.

**Customs**: The seller handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance.

**Typical Use Case**: Conventional (non-containerized) bulk or break-bulk cargo, such as commodities loaded directly onto a vessel, where "alongside the vessel" is a clear, practical handling point.

**Advantages**: Clear, practical delivery point for conventional bulk/break-bulk cargo shipped by sea.

**Risks**: The "alongside the vessel" concept does not translate meaningfully to containerized cargo, which is typically handled at a container terminal well before and separately from the vessel loading process.

**Common Mistakes**: Using FAS for containerized cargo, where the goods are typically delivered to a container terminal (not literally placed alongside a vessel) — an any-mode rule such as FCA is generally more appropriate for containerized shipments.

### FOB — Free On Board

**Informal Category**: "F" (main carriage unpaid by seller).

**Meaning**: The seller delivers when the goods are placed on board the vessel nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment.

**Seller Responsibilities**: Deliver the goods on board the nominated vessel at the named port; clear the goods for export.

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Arrange and pay for main carriage from the port of shipment, and handle import clearance and duties at destination.

**Risk Transfer Point**: When the goods are placed on board the vessel at the named port of shipment.

**Cost Allocation**: The seller bears cost up to the on-board point (including export clearance and loading); the buyer bears main carriage and import clearance/duty costs.

**Transportation Applicability**: Sea and inland waterway transport only.

**Insurance**: No explicit contractual insurance obligation on either party.

**Customs**: The seller handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance.

**Typical Use Case**: Conventional (non-containerized) bulk or break-bulk cargo shipped by sea, historically one of the most widely used and referenced Incoterms in commodity and bulk trade.

**Advantages**: Long-established, widely understood term for conventional sea cargo; clear risk transfer point at vessel loading.

**Risks**: FOB is very commonly and incorrectly used for containerized cargo out of habit and industry convention, even though the goods are typically delivered to a container terminal well before actual vessel loading, creating a mismatch between the term's defined risk point and the shipment's actual practical handling.

**Common Mistakes**: Using FOB for containerized cargo (extremely common in practice, but a well-documented mismatch) rather than the more appropriate FCA rule; citing "FOB" without the required named port of shipment.

### CFR — Cost and Freight

**Informal Category**: "C" (main carriage paid by seller, but risk transfers before destination).

**Meaning**: The seller delivers when the goods are placed on board the vessel, and must contract and pay for freight to the named port of destination, but risk transfers to the buyer at the port of shipment (on board), not at the destination port.

**Seller Responsibilities**: Deliver the goods on board the vessel at the port of shipment; contract and pay for freight to the named port of destination; clear the goods for export.

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Bear risk from the on-board point at the port of shipment, even though the seller is paying freight to the destination port; handle import clearance and duties.

**Risk Transfer Point**: When the goods are placed on board the vessel at the port of shipment — the same risk/cost divergence pattern as CPT (Part B, "any mode" section), applied to sea transport specifically.

**Cost Allocation**: The seller pays freight to the named destination port and handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance/duties.

**Transportation Applicability**: Sea and inland waterway transport only.

**Insurance**: No explicit contractual insurance obligation on either party (contrast with CIF below).

**Customs**: The seller handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance.

**Typical Use Case**: Conventional bulk/break-bulk sea cargo where the seller wants to arrange and pay for ocean freight while limiting its risk exposure to the pre-loading portion of the journey.

**Advantages**: Allows the seller to leverage its ocean freight relationships and rates while limiting risk exposure.

**Risks**: Same risk/cost divergence misunderstanding pattern as CPT — buyers may mistakenly assume risk remains with the seller for the paid ocean voyage.

**Common Mistakes**: Using CFR for containerized cargo rather than the more appropriate CPT; assuming risk transfers at the destination port rather than at the port of shipment.

### CIF — Cost, Insurance and Freight

**Informal Category**: "C" (main carriage paid by seller, but risk transfers before destination).

**Meaning**: Identical in structure to CFR, with the addition that the seller must also contract and pay for cargo insurance covering the buyer's risk from the port of shipment through to the named port of destination.

**Seller Responsibilities**: All CFR responsibilities, plus arranging and paying for cargo insurance at the level of coverage specified by the applicable Incoterms rule text (verify the current specific minimum coverage requirement against the current official ICC rule).

**Buyer Responsibilities**: Bear risk from the on-board point at the port of shipment (as under CFR), while benefiting from the insurance coverage the seller is required to arrange; handle import clearance.

**Risk Transfer Point**: Same as CFR — when goods are placed on board the vessel at the port of shipment.

**Cost Allocation**: Same as CFR, plus the seller bears the cost of the required insurance premium.

**Transportation Applicability**: Sea and inland waterway transport only.

**Insurance**: The seller is contractually required to arrange cargo insurance; verify the current required minimum coverage level against the current official ICC rule text.

**Customs**: The seller handles export clearance; the buyer handles import clearance.

**Typical Use Case**: One of the most widely used and historically referenced terms in international commodity trade, particularly for conventional bulk cargo shipped by sea where the seller conventionally arranges both freight and insurance.

**Advantages**: Long-established, very widely recognized and used in commodity trading; combines freight and insurance convenience for the buyer with limited seller risk exposure.

**Risks**: Same risk/cost divergence misunderstanding as CFR and the other "C" group rules; CIF is also frequently and incorrectly used for containerized cargo, where CIP (the any-mode equivalent) is generally more appropriate.

**Common Mistakes**: Using CIF for containerized cargo rather than the more appropriate CIP; assuming the insurance coverage level required under CIF is comprehensive "all risks" coverage without verifying the specific current minimum requirement, which has historically been defined at a specific, limited level unless the parties expressly agree to a higher level.

# How to Select an Incoterm

## Transportation Mode

If the shipment is containerized or multimodal (combining ocean, rail, road, or air), select from the **any mode of transport** group (EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP) — the sea/waterway-specific rules (FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF) should generally be reserved for conventional, non-containerized bulk or break-bulk sea cargo where their "alongside" or "on board" risk transfer points correspond to a meaningful physical handling point.

## Control

A party wanting greater control over carrier selection, routing, and the transportation process should prefer a rule assigning it responsibility for arranging main carriage — the seller under a "C" or "D" group rule, or the buyer under an "E" or "F" group rule.

## Logistics Capability

Assign responsibility for arranging transportation, insurance, and customs clearance to the party with genuine, demonstrated capability to execute it — a party lacking freight-negotiation relationships, customs expertise, or (for import/export clearance specifically) legal standing in the relevant country should not be assigned that responsibility regardless of which allocation might otherwise seem commercially attractive (see Section 11).

## Risk Tolerance

A more risk-averse party should prefer a rule that transfers risk to the other party at an earlier point in the transaction — a risk-averse seller should prefer an "E" or "F" group rule (transferring risk early); a risk-averse buyer should prefer a "D" group rule (keeping risk with the seller until arrival).

## Insurance

If the parties want the seller to arrange cargo insurance as part of the transaction, only CIP and CIF impose this obligation — for every other rule, each party must independently decide whether and how to insure the portion of transit for which it bears risk (see Section 9).

## Customs Responsibilities

Confirm which party has the legal standing and practical capability to act as importer/exporter of record before agreeing to a rule assigning that party the corresponding customs responsibility — this is a legal/practical capability question, not solely a matter of preference (see Section 11).

## Bargaining Power

The party with greater bargaining power in a negotiation can often secure a rule allocation more favorable to its own risk, cost, and control preferences; a party with less bargaining power should still verify that whatever rule is ultimately agreed does not assign it a responsibility (particularly customs clearance) it cannot actually fulfill, regardless of the overall bargaining outcome.

## Buyer/Seller Location

Consider the relative logistics infrastructure, customs efficiency, and freight market access in both the buyer's and seller's countries — a party located in a country with stronger freight-negotiation access or more efficient customs processes for the relevant leg may be the more logical (not just more powerful) party to hold that specific responsibility, independent of bargaining power alone.

# Incoterm Selection Framework

## Step 1: Identify the Mode of Transport

Determine whether the shipment is containerized/multimodal (favoring the any-mode rules) or conventional bulk/break-bulk sea cargo (where the sea/waterway-specific rules may be appropriate) — see Section 10 and the discussion under each rule in Part B.

## Step 2: Assess Each Party's Logistics and Customs Capability

For both export clearance (seller's country) and import clearance (buyer's country), and for main carriage arrangement, assess which party has genuine legal standing and practical capability — do not proceed to allocate a responsibility to a party lacking this capability, regardless of what the parties might otherwise prefer (Section 11).

## Step 3: Determine Each Party's Risk Tolerance and Desired Control

Establish whether the seller or buyer wants earlier or later risk transfer, and whether either party wants direct control over carrier selection and routing (Sections 5, 7).

## Step 4: Determine Insurance Preferences

Decide whether the parties want the seller to arrange insurance as a contractual obligation (pointing toward CIP or CIF, depending on mode) or whether each party will independently arrange its own coverage for the risk it bears (Section 9).

## Step 5: Cross-Reference Against the Comparison Tables

Use Tables 1-3 above to identify the specific rule(s) consistent with the conclusions from Steps 1-4 — typically narrowing to one or two candidate rules.

## Step 6: Verify the Candidate Rule's Exact Current Obligations

Before finalizing, verify the exact, current obligations of the candidate rule against the ICC's current official Incoterms publication (or with qualified trade counsel) — do not rely on general knowledge, a prior transaction's terms, or this document's conceptual summary alone for the binding, current legal text.

## Step 7: Specify the Named Place/Port and the Incoterms Edition

State the selected rule together with its required named place or port (e.g., "FCA [named place], Incoterms [edition]") and explicitly reference which Incoterms edition applies — citing a rule without both the named place/port and the edition creates avoidable ambiguity (Section 1).

## Step 8: Confirm the Rule Is Reflected Consistently Across All Transaction Documents

Ensure the agreed Incoterm and named place/port are stated consistently in the sales contract, commercial invoice, and transport documents — inconsistency between documents can create disputes or customs clearance delays independent of whether the correct rule was actually agreed upon.

## Step 9: Address What Incoterms Do Not Cover

Separately negotiate and document transfer of title, payment terms and timing, currency, governing law, and dispute resolution mechanism — confirm these are addressed in the sales contract, since the Incoterm itself does not address any of them (Section 4).

## Step 10: Revisit for Future Transactions

Treat the Incoterms edition and rule selection as subject to review for future transactions or contract renewals, since the ICC periodically issues new editions with potentially revised rule content (as has occurred historically, for example with the introduction of DPU replacing an earlier, differently structured rule in a past revision) — always confirm the current edition and rule text at the time of each new or renewed contract, rather than assuming a previously used edition remains current.
