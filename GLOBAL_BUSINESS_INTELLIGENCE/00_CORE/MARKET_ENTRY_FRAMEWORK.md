# MARKET_ENTRY_FRAMEWORK.md

## Global Business Intelligence — Market Entry Framework

*This is an **operational decision-making framework**, not a theoretical essay. It defines, step by step, how Global Business Intelligence must answer the question: "How should this company enter this foreign market?" It operates under [MASTER_INSTRUCTIONS.md](MASTER_INSTRUCTIONS.md) (overall behavior), [RESEARCH_PROTOCOL.md](RESEARCH_PROTOCOL.md) (how to source and verify current information), and [COUNTRY_ANALYSIS_FRAMEWORK.md](COUNTRY_ANALYSIS_FRAMEWORK.md) (invoked directly in Step 4). Every reference to "verify," "current," or "research" below is an instruction to follow RESEARCH_PROTOCOL.md, not an invitation to state a remembered or estimated figure as fact.*

---

## PRIMARY OBJECTIVE

Use this framework to determine:

1. Whether a company should enter a foreign market.
2. Which market should be prioritized (when more than one candidate exists).
3. When the company should enter.
4. Which entry mode should be used.
5. How much control the company should maintain.
6. How much capital should be committed.
7. What risks must be managed.
8. How the company should implement the entry.

These eight questions are answered together, not independently — the entry mode (4) constrains the control level (5) and capital commitment (6); the risk profile (7) shapes the sequencing (Step 13) and the implementation plan (8); the market priority (2) and timing (3) depend on the market and country attractiveness findings (Steps 3–4). Work through Steps 1–15 in order and let each step's output feed the next; do not jump directly to an entry mode recommendation without the preceding analysis.

---

## HOW TO USE THIS FRAMEWORK

1. Execute Steps 1–15 in sequence. Do not skip from Step 1 directly to a recommendation.
2. Scale depth to stakes, per MASTER_INSTRUCTIONS.md Section 8 — a preliminary screen of several candidate markets can move through Steps 1–4 relatively quickly to rank candidates before going deep on Steps 5–15 for the leading candidate only.
3. Apply RESEARCH_PROTOCOL.md to every dynamic data point (market size, growth rates, financial inputs, country conditions). Label every figure as verified fact, current data requiring verification, assumption, or estimate, per MASTER_INSTRUCTIONS.md Section 5.
4. Use [COUNTRY_ANALYSIS_FRAMEWORK.md](COUNTRY_ANALYSIS_FRAMEWORK.md) as the engine for Step 4 rather than duplicating its logic here — that framework already defines, in full, how to evaluate a country; this framework defines how to turn that country evaluation, combined with company- and market-specific analysis, into an entry decision.
5. State explicit assumptions rather than stalling, per MASTER_INSTRUCTIONS.md Section 12, when information is missing but a useful preliminary analysis can still be produced.
6. Populate the [Required Output](#required-output) at the end with the findings from Steps 1–15.

---

## STEP 1 — COMPANY PROFILE

Identify:

- Company
- Industry
- Product/service
- Business model
- Capabilities
- Brand
- Technology
- Intellectual property
- Capital (available for this specific initiative, not total company capital)
- Management capabilities (bandwidth and international experience of the actual team, not just headcount)
- Distribution capabilities
- International experience
- Risk tolerance

**Purpose of this step**: every later step tests the company against the market and country — Step 1 is what gets tested. A weak or incomplete company profile makes every subsequent step's conclusions unreliable, so state explicit assumptions here (per MASTER_INSTRUCTIONS.md Section 12) rather than proceeding on an unstated guess about the company's actual capacity.

---

## STEP 2 — MARKET OBJECTIVE

Identify:

- Revenue objective
- Strategic objective
- Customer objective
- Geographic objective
- Investment horizon
- Expected return
- Desired market position

**Purpose of this step**: distinguishes *why* the company wants to enter this market from *whether* it can. A revenue-driven objective and a strategic (e.g., learning, pre-emption, or platform-building) objective imply different risk tolerance, different entry-mode preferences (Step 6), and different success metrics (Step 14's KPIs) — state which objective type is primary before proceeding, since later steps will otherwise default to evaluating the wrong success criteria.

---

## STEP 3 — MARKET ATTRACTIVENESS

Analyze:

- Market size
- Growth
- Demand
- Profitability
- Customer segments
- Competition
- Pricing
- Barriers
- Regulation
- Market maturity

Distinguish market size, market growth, and overall market attractiveness as three separate constructs (size and growth are inputs; attractiveness is the synthesis judgment that also incorporates competitive intensity and structural profitability potential) — never report size or growth alone as if it settled the attractiveness question. Where more than one candidate market is being compared, produce a ranked, side-by-side view rather than separate, disconnected write-ups.

---

## STEP 4 — COUNTRY ATTRACTIVENESS

Evaluate:

- Political risk
- Economic conditions
- Regulation
- Trade
- Culture
- Logistics
- Labor
- Currency
- Geopolitics

**Use [COUNTRY_ANALYSIS_FRAMEWORK.md](COUNTRY_ANALYSIS_FRAMEWORK.md) when appropriate** — for any market-entry decision of meaningful stakes, run that framework's Steps 1–21 (with the business objective set to "market entry" and the specific product/customer from Steps 1–2 of this framework) rather than re-deriving country-level analysis from scratch here. Bring back into this framework: the Country Score (if produced), the Risk Matrix, the SWOT, and the specific entry-mode-relevant findings (regulatory ownership restrictions, trade access, cultural distance) that Step 6 of this framework will need.

---

## STEP 5 — STRATEGIC FIT

Determine whether the company has:

- Product-market fit
- Competitive advantage
- Resources
- Capabilities
- Brand fit
- Technology
- IP
- Distribution capabilities

**Purpose of this step**: this is the OLI/eclectic-paradigm "ownership advantage" and "internalization advantage" test applied concretely — a company entering a market it does not have genuine strategic fit for should reconsider the objective (Step 2) before selecting an entry mode (Step 6), since no entry mode compensates for a fundamental lack of fit. Test each item specifically against *this* market's competitive set and customer base (from Steps 3–4), not against the company's home-market position — a home-market advantage is not assumed to transfer automatically.

---

## STEP 6 — ENTRY MODE OPTIONS

Consider the full range of modes before narrowing:

- Indirect exporting
- Direct exporting
- Agents
- Distributors
- E-commerce
- Licensing
- Franchising
- Contract manufacturing
- Strategic alliances
- Joint ventures
- Minority investment
- Acquisition
- Greenfield investment
- Wholly owned subsidiary

Screen out modes that are clearly infeasible given Step 4's findings (e.g., a mode requiring full foreign ownership where Step 4 identified an ownership restriction) before conducting the full comparison in Step 7, but do not narrow to a single preferred mode before that comparison — the purpose of this step is to keep the option set genuinely open.

---

## STEP 8 — CONTROL VS. RISK

Explicitly analyze the trade-off between:

- Control
- Investment
- Risk
- Speed

**Do not assume that maximum control is automatically optimal.** State explicitly, for this specific company and market: what does more control actually buy (IP protection, quality/brand consistency, strategic flexibility), and what does it cost (capital exposure, slower market access, loss of a partner's local knowledge)? A company with weak local knowledge (Step 1) and a culturally/administratively distant target market (Step 4) may extract more value from a partner-based mode's local knowledge than from the control a wholly owned mode would provide — state this trade-off in both directions, not only as a justification for the mode already favored.

---

## STEP 9 — PARTNER ANALYSIS

If a local partner is required (by Step 4's regulatory findings, Step 7's comparison, or Step 8's trade-off conclusion), evaluate:

- Strategic fit
- Reputation
- Financial strength
- Distribution
- Local knowledge
- Management
- Culture
- Incentives
- Governance compatibility
- Compliance

Treat each of these ten dimensions with the same rigor as a full partner due-diligence exercise — verify claims independently rather than accepting the partner's own representation of its capabilities, reputation, or distribution reach at face value. Where the partner relationship would take the form of a joint venture, extend this analysis with the full joint-venture partner-selection and due-diligence methodology in the knowledge base's joint ventures reference, including its explicit incentive-alignment mapping (does the partner's actual long-term interest remain aligned with this company's, or does it diverge over time) and its exit/deadlock provisions (Step 15 of this framework should reflect those provisions directly).

---

## STEP 10 — FINANCIAL FEASIBILITY

When data is available, evaluate:

- Initial investment
- Revenue
- Operating costs
- Margins
- Break-even
- Working capital
- NPV
- IRR
- Payback
- Currency exposure

Apply standard capital budgeting methodology (present value discounting at a risk-adjusted rate incorporating the country risk premium from Step 4, explicit separation of project-level from parent-level/repatriable cash flows) rather than a simplified single-year profitability check for any entry mode involving meaningful capital commitment (joint venture, acquisition, greenfield, wholly owned subsidiary). For lower-commitment modes (exporting, licensing, a distributor relationship), a simpler break-even and margin analysis is generally sufficient.

**Do not invent market data or financial figures.** Where the inputs needed for NPV/IRR (a demand forecast, an achievable price point, a local cost structure) are not available, state this explicitly, present the calculation structurally with placeholder variables and a stated method for later population, and flag the specific inputs needed in the final "Information Still Needed" output section — do not substitute a plausible-sounding number to complete the calculation.

---

## STEP 11 — RISK ANALYSIS

Evaluate:

- Market risk
- Political risk
- Regulatory risk
- Currency risk
- Operational risk
- Partner risk
- Cultural risk
- Supply-chain risk
- Reputational risk
- Geopolitical risk

Draw directly on Step 4's country-level risk findings (via COUNTRY_ANALYSIS_FRAMEWORK.md) rather than re-deriving political, regulatory, currency, and geopolitical risk from scratch — this step's distinctive contribution is adding the entry-mode-specific and partner-specific risk categories (operational, partner, reputational, supply-chain) that only emerge once a specific entry mode and partner (Steps 7–9) are under consideration. Present using Low/Medium/High ratings where numerical precision is not justified, consistent with COUNTRY_ANALYSIS_FRAMEWORK.md Step 18's risk matrix convention, and identify mitigation and residual risk for each item.

---

## STEP 12 — SCENARIOS

Create:

- **Base Case** — the most likely trajectory given current, verified conditions and the entry mode/partner configuration under consideration
- **Upside Case** — a plausible, internally consistent scenario more favorable than the base case
- **Downside Case** — a plausible, internally consistent scenario less favorable than the base case

**Explain the assumptions behind each.** Name the specific variables that differentiate the three cases (demand ramp-up speed, competitive response, currency movement, partner performance, a regulatory change) and recalculate the Step 10 financial feasibility view under each case rather than only under the base case. State what observable, monitorable signal would indicate the actual trajectory is departing from the base case toward the upside or downside.

---

## STEP 13 — ENTRY SEQUENCING

Determine whether the company should:

- Enter immediately
- Test first
- Pilot first
- Use a distributor
- Enter digitally
- Establish a local presence
- Scale gradually

Base this decision on the company's international experience and existing market-specific knowledge (Step 1), the market and country distance profile (Steps 3–4), and risk tolerance (Steps 1–2) — a company with limited international experience entering a culturally and administratively distant market should generally favor a staged approach (building market knowledge through a lower-commitment mode before increasing commitment), consistent with incremental internationalization logic, while a company with substantial relevant experience or a genuinely time-sensitive competitive window may justify a faster, higher-commitment entry. State explicitly which of these two situations applies and why.

---

## STEP 14 — IMPLEMENTATION

Create a phased implementation plan:

**Phase 1 — Market Validation**
**Phase 2 — Entry Preparation**
**Phase 3 — Launch**
**Phase 4 — Scale**
**Phase 5 — Optimization**

For each phase, identify:

- Actions
- Resources
- Responsible parties
- Risks
- KPIs

Present as a table per phase, for example:

| Phase | Actions | Resources | Responsible Parties | Risks | KPIs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Market Validation | [e.g., customer discovery, pilot sales, regulatory pre-clearance] | [budget, team, time] | [internal function/role] | [validation risk, cost overrun] | [demand signal thresholds, cost-to-validate] |
| 2. Entry Preparation | [entity/partner formation, licensing, hiring, localization] | | | | |
| 3. Launch | [go-to-market execution, initial sales/production ramp] | | | | |
| 4. Scale | [capacity expansion, channel expansion, team growth] | | | | |
| 5. Optimization | [margin improvement, process refinement, portfolio review] | | | | |

Tie each phase's KPIs back to the objective defined in Step 2 (a strategic-objective-driven entry needs different Phase 1–2 KPIs than a revenue-driven one) and to the scenario triggers defined in Step 12 (a phase gate can explicitly require confirmation that the base case, not the downside case, is materializing before proceeding to the next phase).

---

## STEP 15 — EXIT STRATEGY

Define:

- Failure conditions (the specific, observable conditions that would indicate the entry is not working)
- Exit triggers (the specific thresholds — tied to the KPIs in Step 14 and the downside scenario in Step 12 — that activate a reconsideration)
- Contingency plans (the range of responses short of full exit: re-negotiation, re-positioning, cost restructuring)
- Restructuring options (changing entry mode, e.g., converting a struggling joint venture to a wholly owned structure or vice versa)
- Partner replacement (if the entry involves a partner, per Step 9 — including any contractual buy-sell, right-of-first-refusal, or termination mechanism already in place or that should be negotiated at formation)
- Market exit (the practical and, where relevant, contractual/regulatory mechanics of a full exit, including any host-country approval requirements for divestment)

An entry strategy without an explicit exit strategy is incomplete under this framework — define exit triggers and mechanisms at the same time as the entry plan (Step 14), before capital is committed and before a real underperformance scenario creates pressure to decide these terms reactively.

Notes on populating this template:

- **Executive Summary** is written last, after every other section is complete.
- **Company Profile**, **Market Objective**, **Market Attractiveness**, **Country Analysis**, **Strategic Fit**, **Entry Mode Options**, **Entry Mode Comparison**, **Partner Analysis**, **Financial Feasibility**, **Risk Analysis**, and **Scenario Analysis** correspond directly to Steps 1–12.
- **Recommended Entry Strategy** is the synthesis conclusion from Steps 6–8 (mode), 9 (partner, if applicable), and 13 (sequencing) — state it as a specific, named recommendation (a specific mode, a specific sequencing choice), not a restatement of the comparison matrix.
- **Implementation Plan** and **KPIs** correspond to Step 14 (KPIs may be presented within the phase table or consolidated separately if the user's format prefers a single KPI summary).
- **Exit / Contingency Plan** corresponds to Step 15.
- **Information Still Needed** lists every item flagged for verification throughout the analysis (per RESEARCH_PROTOCOL.md) — market data, financial inputs, regulatory confirmations, partner due-diligence findings — that must be confirmed with a current, authoritative source or direct diligence before capital is committed. This section should not be empty for a real entry decision.
- Omit or condense any section not material to the specific case (e.g., Partner Analysis is not applicable to a wholly owned greenfield entry), but state explicitly that it was scoped out and why, rather than silently deleting it.

---

## IMPORTANT

**Never recommend an entry mode based on one variable.** The recommendation must consider strategic fit, control, investment, risk, speed, scalability, and execution capability together — a mode that scores best on any single dimension (lowest capital, fastest speed, highest control) is not automatically the right recommendation if it scores poorly on the others relative to this specific company's situation. State explicitly how the final recommendation weighs these dimensions against each other for this specific case.

**Do not invent market data or financial figures.** Every market size, growth rate, cost figure, price point, or financial projection used in Steps 3, 7, 10, and 12 must be either a verified, sourced figure (per RESEARCH_PROTOCOL.md), an estimate with its method disclosed, or an assumption explicitly labeled as such — never a plausible-sounding invented number presented as if it were known.
